Can NC State Students Afford to Live Near Campus?

For many NC State students, college marks a new chapter of independence. That often includes a new home. But that independence comes at a price. For students already balancing tuition, food and other expenses, housing can quickly become one of the largest costs of attending college.

Students in their first year are generally required to live on campus, but a majority of NC State undergraduates, 62%, live off campus or commute, according to the University’s Common Data Set. Raleigh rents have declined from their 2022 peaks, but housing around NC State remains expensive.

Thomas Barrie, an NC State professor of architecture who directs the University’s Affordable Housing and Sustainable Communities Initiative and teaches housing policy, said increased construction has helped cool the rental market.

“Rent has gone down. It’s dipped a little bit in downtown Raleigh, and it’s because we’ve actually built a lot of rental housing,” Barrie said. “But it’s still very expensive.”

While the Sept. 9 snapshot of listings from NC State’s Off-Campus Housing site found a lower mean advertised room rent than either single or double residence hall monthly equivalent, the inclusion of utilities and additional fees varies by listing. Those costs may not be reflected in the advertised prices used to calculate the mean.

Alden Hearn, housing navigator for HOST, a Wake County nonprofit serving college students experiencing homelessness or housing insecurity, said those additional charges can substantially change what initially appears to be an affordable rent.

“If you’re a student and you’re thinking, ‘I can relocate to Wake County because I’m seeing places that only cost $700,’” Hearn said, additional charges can change that calculation. “Once you’re leased up and you’ve got the guarantor waiver program and the amenities fee, potentially other fees, it often pushes toward $1,000 a month.”

NC State housing officials said more than 70% of residence hall room inventory is double occupancy. The double room price shown above represents one student’s cost to share a bedroom with another student.

Donna McGalliard, associate vice chancellor and associate dean for Student Life and Advocacy and executive director of University Housing, described double rooms as Housing’s “best kind of value.” She called the comparison between residence hall doubles and per-bedroom prices advertised off campus “apples and oranges,” saying University Housing compares on-campus apartments with off-campus apartments and considers amenities when setting rates.

For students, the practical question may still be which fruit offers more for the price.

Part of that answer lies in what each price includes. Advertised off-campus prices vary in whether they include utilities and additional fees, while NC State’s residence hall rates bundle utilities, laundry, maintenance and student support, although internet is billed through a separate required ResNet fee.

Housing officials also emphasized that NC State students can sign an academic-year housing agreement rather than paying for housing through the summer. Summer housing is separate, and most residents who remain on campus during winter break pay an additional charge.

Peter Fraccaroli, director of Facilities and Business Operations for University Housing, said that shorter contract is one of the advantages of living on campus.

“For us, you can literally arrive in August, you leave at the beginning of May, you’re done,” Fraccaroli said.

McGalliard said the complexity of operating campus housing makes it difficult to reduce its rates to a simple comparison.

“Trying to describe the comprehensive nature of what goes into these numbers,” McGalliard said. “We’re trying to not only adjust for utility costs that we have to pay for, we’re adjusting for, you know, the cost of the elevator contract and the grounds and housekeeping and landscaping and roof contracts. Like all of the things go into setting a rate to include the money to provide activities, the money for the staff that is taking care of the buildings, the ones who are living there, providing the behavioral response, et cetera.”

When asked what specific numerical affordability target University Housing uses when setting rates, officials did not identify one during the interview. Its published projections show that eight of 10 housing options are expected to increase by more than the 3.1% national increase in the Bureau of Labor Statistics’ lodging-while-at-school index from August 2025 to August 2026.

As prices rise, students have to stretch each dollar further. North Carolina’s minimum wage has remained $7.25 an hour since July 2009. NC State’s Federal Work-Study program lists an allowable wage range of $7.25 to $15 an hour, with higher wages requiring a case-by-case exception. The university says work-study students generally should work no more than 20 hours per week, although they may hold another non-work-study job at the same time.

Professor Barrie said he often asks students who are struggling in their architecture studio courses how much they are working and whether they can afford to cut back.

“I’ll say, ‘So how much are you working?’ And they’ll tell me, and I’ll say, ‘Do you need to work that much?’ And then often the answer is, ‘I do.’”

For those students, Barrie said, the answer is to take that financial reality “at face value” and work with it.

To compare advertised room rents with the resources available to a working student, this analysis uses the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development’s 30% housing cost-burden threshold as a reference point. HUD considers households spending more than 30% of their income on housing cost burdened and says they may have difficulty affording other necessities such as food, clothing, transportation, and medical care.

The interactive comparison uses wages ranging from North Carolina’s $7.25 minimum wage to $15 an hour and allows readers to add NC State’s reported average need-based grant or need-based loan award. Because grants and loans are not the same as household income, the 30% line is a reference rather than a formal HUD cost-burden determination.

At $15 an hour and NC State’s general work-study guideline of 20 hours per week, a student would have a gross monthly equivalent of about $1,300. The $862 mean advertised Main Campus room rent would consume about 66% of those wages. Adding the average need-based loan lowers the illustrative share to about 52%, while adding the average need-based grant lowers it to about 34%. Combining both average awards in the hypothetical model brings the share to just over 30%. At 40 hours per week, twice the work-study guideline, the rent would consume about 33% of gross wages without financial aid.

Across the hours modeled, wages between North Carolina’s $7.25 minimum wage and NC State’s standard $15 work-study ceiling never bring the rent below HUD’s 30% housing cost-burden reference. Wages above $15 require a case-by-case exception. Only scenarios that include a need-based grant or loan can fall below that line.

The consequences of those strained housing budgets show up in HOST’s work. The nonprofit began serving students in fall 2022 and became fully operational in early 2023. Since then, “we’ve served just over 100 students,” Hearn said.

For students already stretching their income to afford housing, Hearn said one unexpected expense can be enough to put their housing at risk.

“A lot of students are willing to take a chance on a housing situation where they think they can make ends meet, but a single setback, like if you get sick and you’re out of work for a couple of weeks, or if you lose your job, or if your car breaks down, just falling behind a few weeks or a month of income can basically torpedo the stability of that housing,” Hearn said. “Once you’re evicted, then your options really narrow.”

Hearn called student homelessness “an invisible problem,” in part because people assume that “if someone’s in college, that means that their basic needs are stable.”

“We’ve worked with a lot of students that, maybe because of a popular narrative, expect that they’re going to struggle with housing while they’re in college,” Hearn said. “They might fill in gaps by staying in a vehicle or especially couch surfing with friends if they don’t have family they can return to during breaks.”

Hearn said programs like HOST cannot meet the scale of the need without greater involvement from community and university leaders.

Professor Barrie said that a larger response would require NC State to move beyond emergency programs and invest directly in housing.

“All the resources are good, but they are underfunded for the size of the problem,” Professor Barrie said. “Do the programs, but be a real leader here and actually build the housing.”

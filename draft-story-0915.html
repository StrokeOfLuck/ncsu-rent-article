For many students at NC State, college marks a new chapter of independence. With that comes a new home and, for first-year students, a requirement to live on campus. But that independence comes with a price. For students who are already balancing tuition, food, and other expenses, housing can quickly become one of the bigger costs of attending college.

Most NC State students eventually have to look beyond University Housing. NC State’s Common Data Set reports that 62% of undergraduates live off campus or commute.

Thomas Barrie, an NC State professor of architecture who directs the university’s Affordable Housing and Sustainable Communities Initiative and teaches housing policy, said increased construction has helped cool the rental market.

“Rent has gone down. It’s dipped a little bit in downtown Raleigh, and it’s because we’ve actually built a lot of rental housing,” Barrie said. “But it’s still very expensive.”*

* Raleigh 1- and 2-bedroom rents peaked in 2022 at about 40% above August 2017 levels. By August 2026, both had declined from that peak but remained about 24% above 2017 levels. See References.

While the Sept. 9 snapshot of listings from NC State’s Off-Campus Housing site found a lower mean advertised room rent than either residence-hall monthly equivalent shown above, the inclusion of utilities and additional fees varies by listing.

NC State’s residence hall rates, by contrast, include utilities, laundry, maintenance and student support. Internet is billed through a separate required ResNet fee, which is paid to OIT rather than University Housing.

Housing officials said double rooms are their most common residence hall option. More than 70% of University Housing’s room inventory is double occupancy, meaning two students share a bedroom.

Donna McGalliard, associate vice chancellor and associate dean for Student Life and Advocacy and executive director of University Housing, described double rooms as Housing’s “best kind of value.”

University Housing officials challenged a direct comparison between residence hall doubles and per-bedroom prices advertised off campus.

McGalliard called the comparison “apples and oranges.”

For students, the practical question is what each option includes for the price.

Housing officials also emphasized that NC State students can sign an academic-year housing agreement rather than paying for housing through the summer. Summer and winter-break housing are separate and cost extra.

Pete Fraccaroli, director of Facilities and Business Operations for University Housing, said the shorter contract is one advantage of living on campus.

“For us, you can literally arrive in August, you leave at the beginning of May, you’re done,” Fraccaroli said.

McGalliard said the complexity of operating campus housing makes it difficult to reduce its rates to a simple comparison.

“Trying to describe the comprehensive nature of what goes into these numbers,” McGalliard said. “We’re trying to not only adjust for utility costs that we have to pay for, we’re adjusting for, you know, the cost of the elevator contract and the grounds and housekeeping and landscaping and roof contracts. Like all of the things go into setting a rate to include the money to provide activities, the money for the staff that is taking care of the buildings, the ones who are living there, providing the behavioral response, et cetera.”

When asked what specific numerical affordability target University Housing uses when setting rates, however, officials did not identify one. Its published projections show that eight of 10 housing options are expected to increase faster than the 3.1% 12-month increase in the Bureau of Labor Statistics’ lodging-while-at-school index from August 2025 to August 2026.

As prices rise, students have to stretch each dollar further. North Carolina’s minimum wage has remained $7.25 an hour since July 2009. NC State’s Federal Work-Study program lists an allowable wage range of $7.25 to $15 an hour, with higher wages requiring a case-by-case exception. The university says work-study students generally should work no more than 20 hours per week, although they may hold another non-work-study job at the same time.

Professor Barrie said he often asks students who are struggling in studio how much they are working and whether they can afford to cut back.

“I’ll say, ‘So how much are you working?’ And they’ll tell me, and I’ll say, ‘Do you need to work that much?’ And then often the answer is, ‘I do.’”

For those students, Barrie said, the answer is to take that financial reality “at face value” and work with it.

To compare advertised room rents with the resources available to a working student, this analysis uses HUD’s 30% housing cost-burden threshold as a reference point. HUD considers households spending more than 30% of their income on housing cost burdened and says they may have difficulty affording other necessities such as food, clothing, transportation, and medical care.

The interactive comparison uses wages ranging from North Carolina’s $7.25 minimum wage to $15 an hour and allows readers to add NC State’s reported average need-based grant or need-based loan award. Because grants and loans are not the same as household income, the 30% line is a reference rather than a formal HUD cost-burden determination.

At $15 an hour and 40 hours a week, the $862 mean advertised Main Campus room rent still equals about 33% of gross wages with no aid selected. Adding the average need-based loan lowers the illustrative share to about 29%, while adding the average need-based grant lowers it to about 23%. In both cases, getting below the 30% reference requires adding financial aid to wages.
